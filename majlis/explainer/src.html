RAPTOR, round by round

How does a bus app find your fastest trip in a few milliseconds? It doesn't search a map the way Google Maps searches roads. It reads timetables in rounds: everything you can reach with one bus, then with two, then with three. Pick a starting stop and step through it.

Buses aren't a road map. They're a timetable.

Road routing treats the city as a graph and runs Dijkstra's algorithm over it. Public transport is different: an edge only exists at the moment a bus leaves. RAPTOR, published by Microsoft Research in 2012, starts from that fact. It groups trips into patterns, which are buses that stop at the same places in the same order and never overtake each other. In this city each line has two patterns, one per direction.

Round k means "with at most k buses"

Round 0 is just where you're standing. Round 1 finds everything one bus can get you to. Round 2 starts from every stop that improved in round 1 and finds everything a second bus adds. The rounds stop when nothing improves. Because each round is one more bus, the answer also tells you how many transfers a trip needs.

Each line is scanned once per round

Within a pattern no bus overtakes another, so the first bus you can catch at a stop is also the first to every later stop. RAPTOR walks along the line once, hops on the earliest catchable bus (a binary search over the timetable), and switches to an earlier bus if a later stop on the line lets you catch one. That's why the step counter above stays small: it counts line scans, not individual trips.

Walk after you ride, never twice in a row

After the line scans, each round lets you walk from where a bus dropped you to a nearby stop. The rule matters, and getting it wrong is easy:

The bug the tests caught The textbook version assumes walking links are transitive: if you can walk A→B and B→C, then A→C is listed too. Real walking-radius links aren't. My first implementation sometimes walked onward from a stop it had only reached on foot, chaining two walks into one "transfer". A second, independent algorithm (Connection Scan) disagreed on random cities and exposed it. The fix keeps two times per stop: when a bus can drop you there, and when you can be there at all. Walks start only from the first.

How I know it's right

The Majlis implementation running on this page is checked three ways: hand-worked answers on a tiny real-format (GTFS) feed; 900 random queries on 300 random cities where RAPTOR must agree with Connection Scan at every stop; and 2,821 reconstructed journeys replayed leg by leg to make sure a rider could actually take them. On a city-sized network of 2,025 stops it answers in about 2.6 ms.

Why build it

Majlis uses RAPTOR to find the fairest place for friends to meet by bus: it runs once from each person's home and ranks places by the longest trip anyone has to take, so nobody gets the 70-minute ride. It powers group planning in Lore, a sidequest app for friend groups in Abu Dhabi.
